01 — The Problem

A model that has never read your documents

Picture this: a brilliant new hire, articulate and quick — but nobody has ever given them wiki access. Ask about internal policy and they'll guess, confidently.
🕰️
Knowledge is frozen Everything a model knows was fixed at the moment training ended.
trainedtoday
💭
Gaps get filled with guesses Ask something specific and unknown, and it invents a plausible-sounding answer — a "hallucination."

01 — The Problem

Two tempting fixes — neither scales

📥 Paste every document into the prompt

Models can only read so much text at once.

model's limit

Past the limit, documents simply don't fit — and even short of it, one relevant paragraph gets buried in thousands of irrelevant ones.

🔁 Retrain the model on your documents

Slow and costly — and it goes stale immediately.

🔁

A document changes → the model is out of date again → retrain from scratch. Impractical for docs that update every week.

02 — The Idea

Retrieve → Augment → Generate

🔎 Retrievesearch for the few relevant paragraphs
→
🧷 Augmentattach them to the question as context
→
✍️ Generateanswer using only that context

Nothing is memorized. The model is simply handed the right paragraphs, right when it needs them.

03 — Two Moments in Time

Indexing happens once. Answering happens every time.

🏗️
IndexingDocuments are read, split, and mapped — a one-time (or occasional) setup step
💬
AnsweringA question comes in, the map is searched, an answer comes back — every single time

Everything from here on falls into one of these two moments.

04 — Indexing

Splitting with overlap, so nothing gets cut in half

A document is sliced into fixed-length pieces. Each new piece starts a little before the last one ended.

Why overlap? If an important sentence falls right on a boundary, a clean cut would split it in two. The overlap means it survives whole in at least one piece.

04 — Indexing

Every piece becomes a point on a map

Each piece of text is converted into a list of numbers — a point placed on a map, arranged so pieces about similar topics land near each other, even without sharing exact words.

A question, later, gets placed on this exact same map.

a piece of text

05 — Answering · Step 1

Cast a wide net: nearest neighbors

A question is placed on the same map, then the closest points are pulled out — the pieces most likely to be relevant.

Fast, but a little loose: it's built to avoid missing the answer, even if a stray unrelated point sneaks in.

A closer, slower comparison of question-and-piece pairs re-scores the nearest matches — this only has to run on a short list, never the whole map, which is what keeps it fast.

06 — Answering

The answer is written from the kept pieces only

Example
Answer only using the text below. If it's not in there, say so. Name which piece you used. TEXT: [match A] "...must be at least 12 characters, with one uppercase letter, one number..." QUESTION: what are the requirements?
Result
At least 12 characters, including one uppercase letter, one lowercase letter, one digit, and one special character.
from: match A
